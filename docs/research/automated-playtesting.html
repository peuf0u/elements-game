Elements · playtest research · 28 Oct 2026

How the computer playtests Elements

You asked whether the methods behind the computer players are well known or made up. They're well known. This page shows where each one comes from, what it means in board-game terms, and where it sits in the playtest/ folder.

The short answer

  1. The players are textbook game programs. Looking ahead, skipping hopeless lines, scoring a position by piece values and trying captures first all go back to Shannon (1950) and Knuth & Moore (1975). Every chess engine still does this.
  2. The experiments come from a small research field that uses simulated players to balance games before people test them. Our style players are "restricted play", our ladder is a "strategy ladder", and our exact-range test copies how DeepMind tested chess rule changes with AlphaZero.
  3. The theory underneath: a game like Elements, with no dice and nothing hidden, has a fixed answer under perfect play (White wins, Black wins, or a draw). It's far too big to compute, so we estimate it from many games.
  4. Our own judgment calls: the piece values, the style players' habits, the fortress formations and the warning thresholds. Every result holds only for bots of the strength we used.

1 · The theory

Elements has an answer, we just can't compute it

Zermelo's theorem. In 1913 Ernst Zermelo asked what it means for a chess player to be in a winning position. His result is usually summed up like this: in such a game, either White can force a win, or Black can, or both can force a draw. A historical study notes that this modern version "bears only a partial relationship to what Zermelo really did", and that König and Kalmár finished the proof (Schwalbe & Walker 2001, with an English translation).

For Elements this means your default formation already is, in principle, a White win, a Black win or a draw. The draw rules (repetition and the 50-move rule) guarantee every game ends, so "is it balanced?" has a true answer.

Why we can't compute it. Shannon estimated chess has about 10120 possible games. A machine checking one per microsecond "would require over 1090 years to calculate the first move" (Shannon 1950, full text).

A solved game for comparison. Checkers has about 5 × 1020 positions. Computers worked on it almost non-stop from 1989 to 2007, when Schaeffer's team proved perfect play is a draw (Science, 2007, PDF). Checkers has 32 squares and two kinds of piece. Elements has 81 squares, twelve kinds of element piece, stacks and a Wizard, so solving it is out of reach. Measuring it is the practical route.

2 · The players

Four ways to choose a move

The players climb a ladder: Random, then Greedy (grabs the biggest piece it can), then Careful (looks 2 moves ahead) and Thinker (3 moves ahead). Careful and Thinker use the four methods below.

Looking ahead: minimax

In game terms
"If I play this, what's your best answer, and what's my best answer to that?" Assume the opponent always picks the reply that's worst for you, and choose the move whose worst case is best.
In playtest/
search() in players.mjs. Careful looks 2 turns deep, Thinker 3.
Sources
Shannon proposed cutting this reasoning off after a few moves and scoring the position there (1950), which programmers call his "Type A" strategy. The name and the maths come from von Neumann's minimax theorem (1928). We use the compact form, negamax: your score is minus mine.

Skipping hopeless lines: alpha-beta pruning

In game terms
Once one of your opponent's replies already refutes your move, stop checking their other replies to it. The answer is the same as checking everything, only faster.
In playtest/
The alpha and beta limits in search().
Sources
It finds the same move "without any potential of overlooking a better move" (Chess Programming Wiki). With good move order, a 4-turn search over 40 moves a turn checks about 3,199 end positions instead of 2,560,000. The standard analysis is Knuth & Moore (1975).

Scoring a position: the evaluation function

In game terms
When the bot stops looking ahead, it needs a rule of thumb for who's ahead. Ours adds up piece values (element × size), adds a little for how many moves your pieces have and for attackers near the enemy Wizard, and adds extra when a side is down to its last capturing pieces.
In playtest/
VALUES and evaluate() in players.mjs. There are two value tables, standard and flat.
Sources
This is Shannon's own proposal: material with the 9-5-3-3-1 chess values, plus 0.1 per legal move (full text). He warned the values "only have a kind of statistical validity", which is why we test two tables. Modern engines still start there (Evaluation).

Trying captures first: move ordering

In game terms
Look at "take their biggest piece" before quiet moves. Those are most likely to be best, so alpha-beta can skip more.
In playtest/
orderMoves(): captures by value of the piece taken, then combines, then the rest.
Sources
The classic rule is MVV-LVA: "most valuable victim, least valuable attacker". We use the first half only (Move Ordering).

Random and Greedy are the standard baselines. Jaffe et al. (2012) use exactly these two. Browne warns that random games "don't give much insight into actual quality" (slides, 2022), so we use them only to check that games end.

Variety. A look-ahead bot would play the same game every time. Ours picks at random among moves within 0.1 points of the best. DeepMind solved the same problem by sampling AlphaZero's first 20 moves at random (Tomašev et al. 2020).

3 · The measuring

Each experiment has a name in the literature

Testing a game with simulated players before people play it is its own research topic. Nelson (2011) calls it "hypothetical player-testing": "what happens when the game is played by a player who always attacks… If that player does very well, the game might be a bit too simple." Jaffe et al. (2012) call bot win rates "an early warning system" that saves human playtesting time. That's the job D12 gives playtest/.

Style players are "restricted play"

Jaffe et al. play a bot that's forbidden one thing ("never do a") against a normal bot. If the restricted bot barely loses, that feature doesn't matter much. Our Never-combiner is "never combine" and Combiner is close to "always combine". If Turtle (never crosses the middle) holds off Careful, defence is too strong. One difference: in Jaffe's setup the normal bot knows about the restriction and exploits it, while our Careful doesn't. So our test is gentler than theirs.

The skill ladder measures depth

Lantz, Isaksen, Jaffe, Nealen and Togelius define a game's depth by a strategy ladder: a row of players, each thinking more and each beating the one below. The more rungs, the deeper the game (2017, author copy). Browne's test is the same idea: take one move of look-ahead away from one player, and if the deeper player doesn't win clearly, "the game is most certainly devoid of depth" (thesis pp. 121–122). Our Thinker against Careful is exactly that test. Here are our first results:

Rule variants, the AlphaZero way

DeepMind and former world champion Vladimir Kramnik used AlphaZero to compare nine chess variants, each with one rule changed (no castling, pawns move only one square, and others). For each one they measured draw rate, White's advantage and piece values (Tomašev, Paquet, Hassabis, Kramnik 2020; DeepMind page; chess.com summary). Our exact-range test does the same: the same players and measurements, with one rule changed. Our bots are far weaker than AlphaZero, but the design of the experiment is the same.

Whole-game measures, from the man who let a computer invent a game

For his PhD, Cameron Browne built Ludi, a program that describes board games, plays them against itself, measures them and evolves new ones. Its scores matched how people ranked the games (thesis 2008; Browne & Maire 2010). One of its games, Yavalath, was published commercially. Several of its measures are ours:

  • Completion: how many games end in a win or a loss
  • Balance and advantage: whether one colour or the first player wins more than its share
  • Drawishness: the share of draws
  • Duration: whether games are trivially short or endlessly long
  • Drama: "the tendency for players to recover from seemingly bad positions"; our comeback count measures this

His successor system, Ludii, plays hundreds of board games and publishes the same kind of table for each one (example; Piette et al. 2020). His 2022 slides sum up a good game's first test: fair, decisive, and a good length.

The handicap test is an odds game

Larry Kaufman worked out chess piece values from results. He took nearly 300,000 master games and compared results with ratings when material was unequal. That gave pawn 1, knight and bishop 3¼, rook 5, queen 9¾ (Chess Life, 1999). Our handicap test does the same from the other side. Careful plays with one piece removed, and the bigger the drop in its score, the more that piece is worth in practice.

The ± ranges

A win rate from 100 games is only an estimate. The range next to each number is a 95% Wilson interval, the standard formula for how far off a percentage could be. If the range includes 50%, the games can't tell the two sides apart yet.

4 · Our own judgment

What isn't from any source

  • Piece values. Both tables are guesses. The handicap test checks them.
  • The other scoring weights: mobility, pressure on the Wizard, the bonus for the last capturing pieces.
  • Look-ahead depths of 2 and 3, chosen so thousands of games run in minutes on a laptop.
  • The style players' habits. The method is established, but which habits to test is our guess about where cheats might hide.
  • The fortress formations, hand-built worst cases for defence.
  • The warning thresholds and how many games each test gets.
5 · Limits

Results hold for bots of this strength

Even in chess, results shift with strength: as AlphaZero thought longer, draws rose and White's edge shrank (Tomašev 2020). Weak bots can hide an imbalance by never finding the attack a strong player would. They can also invent one by walking into a trap any person would see. So treat a "cheat" the bots find as a hint to check on the board, not a verdict.

  • The scoring steers the bots. A bot that thinks Earth is worth 1 plays as if it is.
  • Bots don't adapt. A person who loses to a Turtle twice changes plans. Careful doesn't.
  • No placement phase yet. Games start from finished formations.
  • Stronger players are possible, at a cost. Monte Carlo tree search judges moves by playing many quick games and needs no piece values (Coulom 2006, Kocsis & Szepesvári 2006, survey 2012). Self-taught play like AlphaZero is the strongest option, but its chess training used 5,000 of Google's AI chips (preprint). A home-sized version for Elements would be a project of its own.
  • People still decide. Bots measure fairness, length and dominant strategies, not fun or tension. Nelson presents his methods as a complement to human playtesting, not a replacement.
