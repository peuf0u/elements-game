Elements · computer playtest · 28 Oct 2026 · about 120,000 games

What the computer learned playing Elements

The core game is healthy: fair, decisive, with real depth and no unbeatable defence. Water is the strongest element. The first, weak bots made it look overwhelming and Fire useless, but a much stronger bot shows every attacking piece matters. If you want the elements even, one small rule change does it: Water captures only straight.

Score counts a win as 1 and a draw as ½, so 50% means even. Numbers in brackets are the 95% uncertainty range: if it includes 50%, the games can't tell the two sides apart.

One move means one player's turn. All results are for bots that look 2–3 moves ahead, roughly a careful club player who doesn't plan. Treat anything that looks like a problem as a hint to try on the real board.

Searching for better formations

A search tried about 100 formations in about 5,000 games: variations of your default, random setups, and hand-made ones with Fire moved forward. Each round it kept the best eight and varied them again. At the end the top six played each other and your default.

Nothing beats your default. It scored best in the final round robin (53%), and the six finalists scored 45–51%. Early on some formations scored up to 67%, but that was luck from only 30 games. What the search does show is that many formations are about equally good: a set of good starting formations is easy to offer, and a player's setup won't decide the game. All six finalists moved Fire 2 forward, yet Fire still made only 0.4–0.75 captures per game, against Water's 2.0–2.4.

9 · Water rules

One rule change evens out the elements

Two variants, both switchable in the rules:

  • No edge slide: Water can't turn along the board edge, only around Earth.
  • Straight captures: Water still flows and bends around Earth to get somewhere, but the step that lands on an enemy piece must be a straight flow along the column.

Straight captures even out the elements. Water, Wind and Fire each give about a third of the checkmates, and their big pieces are worth about the same. For the first time, losing Fire 3 costs something. So Fire was never broken: Water's bent and sideways captures simply did its job better. No edge slide alone is a small fix: Water just bends around the attacker's own Earth instead, and still gives three quarters of the checkmates.

10 · A stronger bot

Deep: chess-engine techniques, learned piece values

Careful and Thinker look a fixed 2 or 3 moves ahead with guessed piece values. Deep uses the standard techniques of chess engines:

  • It looks one move deeper at a time until its budget runs out.
  • It remembers positions it has already searched.
  • It tries the most promising moves first.
  • It follows captures and checks to the end of its look-ahead.
  • It treats a repeated position as a draw.

Its piece values are learned: from 4,000 games of Deep against itself, the values that best predict who went on to win. Relative to the average attacking piece (= 1):

  • The sizes of effects are understated: a missing pawn costs strong humans about 100 rating points, these bots about 20.

13 · Move limit

75 moves each instead of 50

Most draws between strong bots came from the 50-move rule. At those draws only about 3 capturing pieces are left in total, and more than half of the last 100 moves are Wizards and plain Earth shuffling. Earth never leaves the board, so a few remaining attackers can't break its walls.

A second formation search with Deep 2k found eight strong, varied formations. Your default came last of nine, and Wind 3 held back came first, so it is the app's new default, with five presets beside it. The boards are on the formations page (playtest/FORMATIONS.html).

  1. Give each Water an Earth to lean on. This is the strongest rule, and it holds everywhere. Water bends around Earth, so an Earth just ahead gives it routes to both sides, and it blocks attacks aimed at the Water.
  2. Keep the pieces close together, so they can combine early.
  3. Water on the middle row, Earth on the front row: the Earth walls the front, and the Water waits behind it.
  4. No Earth directly in front of your Wizard. It probably takes the Wizard's escape square and gives enemy Water a post to bend around next to it, as with the Earth shield in section 8. This one rests on only 26 formations.
  5. Keep Wind 3 back: the main change from your default to Wind 3 held back.

What didn't matter: the Wizard on row 1 or further forward, on the e-file or not, the Winds' square colours, and where Fire, Fire 3 or Water 3 stand.

These rules are only part of the story. Together, the 23 features measured explain about a quarter of the real differences between formations, and among formations near the presets only about a tenth. A formation's strength comes mostly from how its pieces work together: which covers which, and where the Water can go. That can't be read off a checklist and has to be found by playing. A player who builds a formation from scratch will rarely find one as strong as the presets, so presets are worth offering, and the placement phase rewards experience.

15 · Rules v0.3

Water bends diagonally, one way per move

Playing the prototype, you found Water's attacks hard to see and its reach too large. After comparing ten rules (the Water rules page), you chose diagonal bends with one way per move, keeping the edge slide. Water still flows along its column. Its steps around Earth must be diagonal, and within one move it goes only forward or only backward.

Every piece's value

From shared random formations, by size 3 / 2 / 1: Water 20 / 37 / 38%, Wind 31 / 38 / 38%, Fire 27 / 36 / 42%, Earth 43 / 54 / 54%. Water 3 still matters most there, so Water stays the strongest element, by a smaller margin.

Fair, decisive, and combining matters more

  • White's edge stays small: 52% from the default formation and from random ones.
  • Draws: 22% from the default formation (24% with v0.2), 32% from random ones.
  • Playing styles against a plain Careful bot: Turtle 31%, Rusher 28%, Hunter 43%, Never-combiner 13%, Combiner 65% (51% with v0.2). No habit wins outright, but combining now pays off more, probably because a single Water reaches less.

Formations under v0.3

In the presets' round robin, Wind 3 held back comes first, about 35 rating points above Fire in front of the Wizard. A new formation search under v0.3 found one formation about as good: Fire forward, your original with Fire 2 in front of the Wizard and Fire 1 out on h2. It beat Wind 3 held back 57% with Deep 2k and 53% with Deep 20k: level, within luck.

What this means, and your questions

  1. The core is healthy: fair, decisive, deep, no unbeatable defence, and games of a good length. The first-move edge is a little larger than chess's at the same level, and draws more common.
  2. Water leads, but every attacking piece matters with strong play. Formations can't change Water's lead. Straight captures even the elements out, at the cost of longer games and a few more draws, so it's a matter of taste.
  3. Both ways to win matter: checkmate decides about half the games between strong bots, and elimination a quarter to a third.
  4. Exact range makes the game slower and more drawish. It stays off by default.
  5. Formations matter a little and are hard to build well. Your default came last with strong play, and the new default and presets are clearly better. No formation wins by itself, and mirror images are just as good. Presets are worth offering, and the placement phase rewards experience.

These are recorded as open questions 6–9 in GAME_SPEC.md, plus setup safety and bot difficulty in PRODUCT_BRIEF.md. The biggest one is a rule to decide: whether Water may capture at the end of a bent route. The computer's answer is to try "straight captures" at the table.

Limits

What these results can't tell us

  • Bot strength. Section 11 shows how much it matters: "Fire is useless" came from weak bots. Deep is much stronger but still doesn't plan, so a strong human may see things it can't.
  • No placement phase. Games start from finished formations.
  • Sample sizes. Most Thinker results are 50–100 games each, so differences under about 10 points could be luck. Section 9 shows how easily: 60 games said 37%, and 200 said 52%.
  • Bots repeat themselves. A bot that always picks its best move can replay one game over and over, so the Thinker games in section 9 start with random first moves.
  • Piece values: Careful and Thinker use guesses; Deep's are learned from games.
